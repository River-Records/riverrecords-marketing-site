A patient with type 2 diabetes, albuminuria and an eGFR that has drifted down over three years. Not on an ACE inhibitor. You notice it halfway through the visit, and the obvious thought arrives: why not?

Ask the literature and you get a clear, correct answer. A patient with diabetes and albuminuric kidney disease should generally be on an ACE inhibitor or an ARB, unless there's a reason not to be.

The whole question is in that last clause. Somewhere in this patient's chart, there probably is a reason. Maybe the potassium went to 5.9 two months after lisinopril was started, the drug was stopped, the potassium came back down, and the nephrologist agreed it should stay off. That decision was made carefully, by people who knew the patient. It's recorded in one sentence of a note from 2023, and in a consult letter filed under a different date.

The guideline answer is right about the population. It also happens to be the one thing you've already tried.

## Concede first: the new tools are useful

In the last few months, some AI scribe vendors have announced a place to ask clinical questions beside the note: a literature search, a reference lookup, a chat box that cites its sources. I want to say plainly that this is a good thing.

Looking something up mid-visit used to mean a second window, a search bar and a guess about which source would load fastest. Putting a well-sourced answer one click from where you're already working is a real improvement. If it gets more clinicians checking a dose, an interaction or an updated recommendation, rather than going from memory, patients are better off. Plenty of good physicians will use these tools every day, and they should.

So this isn't an argument against them. It's an argument about which question they answer.

## Two kinds of question

There are, roughly, two kinds of question you ask during a visit.

The first is about medicine. What's the first-line agent? What's the screening interval? Does this drug interact with that one? These questions have general answers, the answers live in the literature, and a good reference tool returns them quickly.

The second is about this patient. Why was the ACE inhibitor stopped? What did the last nephrology visit conclude? Why aren't we screening for colon cancer anymore? Did we already try the second agent, and what happened? These questions have answers too, but no textbook holds them. They're decisions, made by you or a colleague, with reasons, at a specific point in a specific person's care.

In my experience the first kind is rarely where a visit stalls. Most of us know the first-line agent. We know the screening interval, or we know exactly where to look it up. Where we stall is the second kind. Not because the medicine is hard, but because the answer is a fact about our own past work and we can't find it.

## Why the second question is harder

A decision about a patient doesn't live anywhere in particular. It lives in the assessment and plan of whichever visit it was made in, phrased however it was phrased that day, in a note written for that visit and not for this one.

So finding it is archaeology. You open notes in reverse order and scan each plan for the problem you care about, while the patient waits. I've written before about [how much of chart review is hunting rather than reading](/blog/i-dont-read-charts-i-hunt-through-them/). This is the sharpest version of it, because the thing you're hunting for isn't a lab value with a date. It's a reason, and reasons don't come with dates.

When the hunt fails, one of three things happens, and none of them shows up as an error.

**You re-derive it.** You work the problem from first principles and arrive, eventually, at the conclusion someone already reached. That's time and judgment spent producing something the chart already contained.

**You re-litigate it.** You reopen a decision that was settled, with the patient, for good reasons you can't see. The patient who agreed two years ago to stop screening after a long conversation now has to have the conversation again.

**You reverse it.** You follow the guideline and restart the drug that was stopped for a reason, without knowing the reason. The literature tool didn't steer you wrong. It answered the question you asked. The question was the wrong one.

## Why no literature can hold this

None of this is a flaw in the reference tools. A literature answer is, by design, an answer about people in general. Its quality comes from being drawn from many patients and not from one.

The decision you need was drawn from one. It depends on a potassium value, a conversation, a specialist's view, a patient's preference, a side effect nobody coded. It's not in any guideline because it shouldn't be. It's the part of care that *is* this patient.

This is why I'd be careful about treating a smarter question box as progress on the problem that actually slows a visit down. Any assistant that answers from the literature, however good, is answering the first kind of question. The second kind can only be answered from the patient's own record. And in most practices that record holds the answer in a form that's expensive to get back out: scattered across years of encounter notes, each organized around the visit it came from rather than the problem it was about.

## What the record is for

None of this is a complaint about the EHR. The record does its job. It holds every note, every result and every letter, and it holds them reliably. The decision about the ACE inhibitor is in there. Nothing was lost.

The trouble is that a record built to keep everything isn't automatically built to bring back the one thing you need. Clinicians who carry patients closely solve this by hand. They keep a running line at the top of their notes, "ACEi stopped 2023, K 5.9, nephro agrees, do not restart", because they've learned that otherwise the decision has nowhere to live. It's a good habit, and I wrote about it in [The Running Summary](/blog/the-running-summary/). It also depends on someone remembering to write the line and carry it forward, every visit, indefinitely.

The question worth asking isn't how to look things up faster. It's how a decision made about a patient, with its reason attached, can stay findable by the problem it belongs to, so the next clinician to open the chart, or you in eight months, starts from what was already decided rather than from the guideline.

## The question worth answering

The new tools are good at telling you what medicine knows. That has real value, and I'm glad they exist.

But most of the hard thinking in primary care is about what *we* know about *this* person, which we worked out over years and wrote down one visit at a time. That knowledge is the most specific clinical information that exists about the patient in front of you. It deserves to be as easy to reach as a guideline.

*Next time a visit stalls, notice which kind of question stalled it. Was it about medicine, or about this patient?*
