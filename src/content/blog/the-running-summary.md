Open last month's note. Copy the block at the top. Update the A1c, add the line from the cardiology letter, delete the knee pain that resolved in the spring. Sign.

If you keep a running summary, you know the block. It has a shape only you would have chosen: active problems in the order you worry about them, the decision you don't want anyone to re-litigate, the med that was stopped and why. Nobody taught you to keep it. At some point you noticed you were re-reading the same three notes before every visit with the same patient, and you decided to stop doing that.

## Concede first: it works

Plenty of good clinicians keep one for every patient they carry closely, and their patients are better for it. A well-kept summary is the fastest way back into a patient you haven't seen in eight months. It holds the things the chart technically contains but never presents: the context around a decision, the reason a plan looks the way it does, what you were waiting on.

This is good medicine. I want to say that plainly before saying anything else, because copy-forward is usually discussed as a vice.

## Why you had to build it

A note is written for one visit. It is excellent at that job. Once it is signed, though, nothing in it stays pinned, and nothing in it is quick to review. The next visit starts with a blank note, and the patient's problems have not reset.

So the summary has to travel, and copy-forward is how it travels. The only durable place to put something you want to see next time is the note you're writing now, and the only way to get it into next time's note is to carry it there.

## The upkeep

A running summary only works if three jobs get done at every visit.

**Re-type it.** Or re-paste it and walk through it line by line, because a summary you paste without reading is no longer yours.

**Prune it.** Delete what has resolved, collapse what has settled, demote what no longer drives a decision.

**Carry it forward.** Into the next note, and the one after that, indefinitely.

The three jobs don't cost the same. Adding a line is cheap: the new result is in front of you and you type it in. Pruning is expensive, because deleting a line means deciding it no longer matters, and that decision needs the same judgment as the rest of the visit. When the schedule is full and the inbox is worse, the pruning is what goes. The paste still happens. The judgment about what to keep waits for a quieter day, and on a hard week that day doesn't come.

So the summary grows. It doesn't grow because anyone stopped caring. It grows because the cheap job got done and the expensive one got deferred, which is how any sensible person triages a bad afternoon.

## What the duplication measures

In a 2022 study in JAMA Network Open, Jackson Steinkamp, Subha Airan-Javia and I [looked at duplication across 104,456,653 clinical notes](/research/), written for 1.96 million patients at a large academic health system. Half the text in the record, 50.1%, was duplicated word for word from prior documentation. Roughly half of that was clinicians copying their own earlier notes. The other half was clinicians copying from someone else's.

We concluded that duplication at that scale isn't a mistake individual clinicians make. It is a product of the documentation system, and of a note paradigm that asks for a fresh document at every encounter.

I now read the number one step further. Much of that duplicated half is the visible cost of upkeep. Many of those copied lines are someone carrying something forward because there was nowhere else to keep it. The half copied from other clinicians' notes may be the most telling part: people borrowing a colleague's summary because that's where the patient's picture lived.

## Why the usual advice doesn't land

The standard guidance is to copy forward only what is stable and rewrite the rest. We've given that advice on this blog ourselves, in [The Real Cost of Note Bloat](/blog/the-real-cost-of-note-bloat/), and as advice about the note it is right.

It says nothing about where the summary should live instead. Telling a clinician to stop carrying it, without giving it somewhere to stay, is telling them to stop keeping it. The good ones won't, and they shouldn't. What they'll do is keep paying the upkeep, and keep getting blamed for the part that shows.

## The better question

"How do we get clinicians to copy forward less?" is the wrong question. The useful one is why a patient's summary needs carrying at all. That is the problem of information persistence, which I've written about in [The Note Is Solved. The Chart Is Not.](/blog/the-note-is-solved-the-chart-is-not/): a fact you need on every visit shouldn't have to be moved there by hand.

Until that problem is solved, the running summary is the best tool most of us have, and the clinicians who keep one well are doing something worth respecting. If the duplication bothers you, put the blame on the system that made the summary need carrying.
