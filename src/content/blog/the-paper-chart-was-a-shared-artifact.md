In one American ICU, described in [a 2003 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1480222/) while it still ran on paper, each patient's chart was a binder. When a physician wrote new orders in it, the binder went into the "Chart In" box beside the unit secretary's desk. She pulled the orders, transcribed them, and sent them where they needed to go. A held order got a stroke of yellow highlighter on the medication record. The study describes one ambiguous order being settled at her desk, by a convention the unit shared: you go by the most recent order.

I keep coming back to that binder. It was one object, and it stood for the patient. Physicians wrote in it, nurses wrote in it, and the pharmacist and the secretary worked from it. An informatics group studying a university clinic's paper records called what they found a "[multiauthored medical record system](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2247843/)." The chart was shared, and I think that word matters more than we have noticed.

## The binder had a keeper

On that unit I'd call the secretary its keeper. That is my word and not the study's: what it documents is that a chart with new orders came to her desk, that she transcribed them and sent them on, and that her desk was the unit's "hub" for questions and answers. As I understand it, unit clerks more generally were the ones who assembled the chart, filed what came in and thinned it when it got too thick to use, and a medical records department took it after discharge to put it back in order and chase what was missing. I can't document that the way I can the ICU, so take it as my understanding and not as history.

I should be careful here. That work was structural. I haven't found anyone describing the clerk as editing what the chart said, and I doubt they did. But the structure, as I understand it, was theirs. I'd expect the order of a chart varied from place to place, according to whoever used it there.

The people came through too. The ICU study records the unit secretary "learning about individual providers' idiosyncrasies" as part of the job. I imagine it went further than that. I'd guess certain clinicians were allowed habits that others weren't, and that you could pick up some charts and tell whose patients they were. That part is my guess. The study documents the habits, not the permission.

That is the thing I mean: an artifact shaped by the place and the people who used it.

## The note was always one person's

My idea, when I first said it out loud, was that the chart was shared and the note is singular. That's too neat.

A progress note on paper was written and signed by one person, and one name signs it now. An [earlier post here](/blog/what-next-in-medical-charts/) made that point about the paper note, and it was right. And the electronic chart has no shortage of hands in it. Many people read and write in any chart I open.

So the difference isn't the number of authors. On the unit I've described there was one binder, and one person I've called its keeper. What we have now, as I see it, is a pile of notes, each reflecting its author's understanding of the patient on the day it was written, and nobody whose job is the whole. In [one hospital studied before and after its EHR](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26432683/), clinicians described losing a shared understanding of the patient across professions. That is a single site, but it is the loss I recognize.

## What paper cost

None of this is a case for the binder.

In that same university clinic, physicians [could not find all the information they wanted](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2247843/) in the chart in 81 percent of cases, and the authors concluded that the many-authored record did not give them effective access to it. Primary care clinicians in Colorado [reported important clinical information missing at 13.6 percent of visits](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15687311/), about half of it held somewhere outside their own clinical system. In one Texas hospital, [a fifth of handwritten medication orders](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9090521/) were illegible or legible only with effort. Each of those is one study in one place. Together they describe a record that often failed the people reading it.

And what replaced it bought something real. Entering a medication order by computer [cuts the likelihood of an error on that order by about half](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23425440/), 48 percent in one pooled analysis. The same authors say it is unclear whether that carries through to less harm for patients, and we should be honest about the alerts: clinicians [override somewhere between half and nearly all of them](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16357358/). Still, an order entered by computer is legible, and it is checked against the patient's recorded allergies before it goes anywhere. I would not give that back, and I don't know a clinician who would.

I think some of the art of medicine went out with the binder, and I think that's okay. The safety gains are critical, and if I had to choose I would choose them again.

## Keeping the safety, getting the rest back

But I don't think we have to choose. Nothing I have read says the adaptiveness was the price of the safety. As I read it, they went at the same time, for different reasons. Structured ordering needed structure. The rest of the chart didn't have to lose its keeper, and as far as I can see it did.

It's telling that paper never quite left. A study of fourteen American sites that had gone electronic [found paper still in widespread use at every one](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20351841/). I read that as people still building the local, shared thing by hand, because they need it.

What has changed, I think, is that software can now read and organize ordinary clinical prose, which is what the chart was always made of. That makes it possible to imagine the keeper's job being done again: something that holds the whole, alongside the EHR that keeps the record and the safety, and that takes its shape from the patient, the clinicians, and the culture of the practice that uses it.

That is not going back to paper. It's a marriage of technology and humanity that hasn't really been possible before, and I think it could be a beautiful one.
