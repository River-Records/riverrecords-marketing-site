A follow-up visit for a patient with type 2 diabetes and hypertension. The assessment and plan reads:

> Diabetes: stable. Continue metformin.
>
> Hypertension: stable. Continue lisinopril.
>
> Labs reviewed. Follow up 3 months.

Now put a coding suggestion beside it. Whatever produces that suggestion, it has one thing to work from, and it is those four lines. Two chronic problems are named, so the problems element may reach moderate. Nothing else does. No data is identified. No risk is stated. A careful suggestion says 99213, and over that note it is right.

The visit may not have been a 99213. The A1c had gone from 7.1% to 7.9%. You asked about adherence, considered adding a GLP-1, deferred it for cost and because there was no cardiovascular indication yet, and increased the metformin. That is decision-making, and a good part of it is prescription drug management. It happened in the room. It is not in the note, so nothing that reads the note can find it.

That is the whole argument of this post. A code suggestion can only be as good as the note under it.

## The suggestions are coming, and they will be useful

E/M code suggestions are arriving inside documentation tools, and I want to say first that this is reasonable and mostly welcome. In [the first part of this series](/blog/the-undercoding-tax/) I described the cognitive cost of coding accurately in the moment: holding a visit's complexity in your head while you finish the note, see the next patient and answer the message that just came in. The familiar middle code is the path of least resistance. Something that does the counting for you, at the moment you sign, addresses a real cause of undercoding.

These tools will also improve. Assume they get very good at applying the rules. Assume every one of them reads the 2021 guidelines correctly, counts correctly and never invents an element. The question that remains doesn't change as they improve: what are they counting?

## What a suggestion can see

Since January 2021, the level of an office visit is chosen by medical decision-making or by total time. History and exam still have to be performed and documented as medically appropriate, but they no longer count toward the level. When the level is set by MDM, there are three elements: the **problems addressed**, the **data reviewed and analyzed**, and the **risk** of the patient's management. You need to meet the level in two of the three.

Each of those is a claim about what you did, not about what is in the chart:

- A problem is **addressed** when you evaluated or treated it at this encounter. Listing it is not addressing it. Noting that another clinician manages it is explicitly not addressing it.
- **Data** counts when it is specific. "Labs reviewed" counts for nothing you can defend. Which test, from when, and what you concluded.
- **Risk** is the risk of the management, including options considered but not chosen. Starting, stopping or changing a medication, or a documented decision to continue one with its monitoring, is prescription drug management, and that is the workhorse of moderate risk in primary care.

A suggestion can only meet those definitions with what the note supplies. It cannot see that you considered a GLP-1 unless the note says so. It cannot tell that a problem was worked on today if the only trace of it is a line carried from March. Everything it knows about your decision-making, it learned from the page.

## Two ways the same gap goes wrong

When the note doesn't carry the MDM, a suggestion over it fails in one of two directions, and both of them are the note's fault.

**The undercode.** The note is thin, as above. The suggestion reads it accurately and lands below the care delivered. You may even feel reassured, because the software agreed with the cautious code you would have chosen anyway. Nothing about that is wrong as coding. It is the undercoding problem from part 1, now with a second opinion confirming it.

**The audit risk.** The note is long but doesn't show the work. A problem list carried into the note looks like several problems. "Labs reviewed" looks like data. Three paragraphs copied forward from the last visit look like complexity. A suggestion that counts what it sees could land on a higher level, and the note would not survive someone checking it, because a problem list carried in is not a set of problems addressed, unnamed data is not data, and a reviewer reading copied text cannot separate what you did today from what you did in March. In [the second part](/blog/your-denials-start-in-the-note/) I argued that a claim is adjudicated on the record of the care, not on the care. A code is no different, and a suggested code is no different either.

In both cases the suggestion may be a fair reading of the document. The document is the problem.

## The same visit, documented so it can be read

Here is that visit again, written the way the guide on [documenting medical decision-making](/guides/the-defensible-visit/) works it through:

> Type 2 diabetes: A1c 7.9% (today), up from 7.1% in April. Adherence good by report. Considered adding a GLP-1; deferred given cost and no CV indication yet. Increasing metformin to 1000mg BID. Recheck A1c in 3 months.
>
> Hypertension: BP 142/90 today, similar to last two visits. Reviewed home readings patient brought (avg 138/86). Continuing lisinopril 20mg; will add amlodipine if not improved by next visit. BMP today given dose considerations.

Same visit, same care. The problems element is two chronic illnesses, one with progression: moderate. The risk element is prescription drug management, stated rather than implied: moderate. Two of three, which is all a 99214 needs.

The data element is worth slowing down on, because it is where a suggester's competence would show. It does not carry this visit. Reviewing a result and ordering a test is two items, and Category 1 wants three. The home readings are patient-supplied data, which is contested. Some coders count it and some don't, and a visit that only reaches its level by counting a disputed item isn't defensible. This one doesn't need to. The right reading, from a person or a program, is "moderate on problems and risk; data not met," and the level stands without it.

That note is longer than the first, but not by much, and the extra length is all decision-making. It is not a fuller review of systems or a longer exam. It is the reasoning that was already in your head, written down. Once it is written down, any honest suggestion over it lands in the right place, and so does any honest auditor.

## The fix is upstream

So the place to work is not the suggester. A more sophisticated suggestion over the first note is still reading four lines. It can only guess at what isn't there, and a guess is the last thing you want behind a level.

The place to work is the structure of the note. A traditional note pours the visit into one block organized by time, and the MDM is smeared across it: the problem in one place, the result in another, the decision implied somewhere in between. When the note is organized by problem, each problem carries its own evaluation, the data that bore on it, and what was decided and why. That is the same unit the MDM table counts. Problems addressed are the problems with work under them. Data is named against the problem it informed. Risk is visible because the medication decision sits under the problem it was made for.

That is the shape a reviewer needs, and it is the same shape a suggestion needs. Get the note right and the counting becomes the easy part, whoever does it.

## What I'd ask of any coding suggestion

If your documentation tool offers a level, a few questions are worth asking of it, and of your own notes:

- **Can it show which lines of the note support each element?** A level you can't trace to the text is a level you can't defend.
- **Does it distinguish addressed from listed?** A problem list carried into the note should not count as problems addressed.
- **Will it say when an element isn't met?** "Data not met; level supported by problems and risk" is a more trustworthy answer than one that reaches for a third element.
- **Does it ever suggest lower than you expected?** One that only ever confirms or raises your instinct deserves a second look.
- **Does the decision stay with you?** The suggestion is an input. The code is the clinician's and the practice's call.

None of those is about how clever the suggestion is. They are about whether it is reading a note that shows the work.

## The line this argument does not cross

This is not an argument for billing a higher level. Billing a level you didn't earn is fraud, and the caution clinicians feel about it is well placed and worth keeping. A tool that treats a suggested level as a target to reach, or a note as something to fill until the target is met, is building the audit risk described above on purpose.

The goal is documentation that reflects the work. Where the work was a 99213, the note should say so plainly and the suggestion should agree. Where it was a 99214, the note should show the problems, the data and the risk well enough that anyone reading it later reaches the same answer. In [part 3](/blog/risk-adjustment-documentation-structure/) I put it as a risk score that matches the patient, no higher and no lower. The same goes for an E/M level. It should match the visit.

Coding help will keep getting better. It will only ever be as good as the note under it, and the note is still ours to write.
